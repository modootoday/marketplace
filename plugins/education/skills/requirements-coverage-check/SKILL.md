# Requirements coverage check

A draft can read well and still skip a requirement. This skill answers one question: for each thing the
brief asks, where does the draft deliver it?

## Steps

1. Read the brief or rubric first, before the draft. Number every requirement as written, splitting a
   sentence that asks for two things into two numbers (for example "define X and give two examples" is
   R1 and R2). Keep the brief's own wording for each. If the brief is ambiguous, list the readings you
   will use and ask the user to confirm.
2. Read the draft with paragraph, section or line numbers. If it has none, number paragraphs yourself
   and say so.
3. For each requirement, search the draft and record one of three statuses.
   - Covered: the draft does everything the requirement states. Quote the words and give the location.
   - Partial: the draft touches it but misses a stated part (a count, a data point, a named element,
     a comparison). Quote what is there and name exactly what is missing.
   - Uncovered: nothing in the draft addresses it. Say which locations you searched.
4. Judge by the requirement's own words, not by effort or quality. A requirement that asks for two
   strategies is partial with one. A mention in passing that does not do what the verb asks
   (discuss, compare, justify) is partial.
5. Present a matrix: requirement number, requirement text, status, location, quoted evidence, what is
   missing. Follow it with counts (covered, partial, uncovered) and the requirements to fix first.
6. Do not rewrite the draft, supply missing paragraphs or reword sentences unless the user asks. You
   may say what kind of content would satisfy a missing requirement in one line.
7. State what you did not check: factual accuracy, citation quality, argument strength and grade.
   Whether the work passes is for the instructor or the author.

## Output

The numbered requirement list with any ambiguity noted, the matrix, the three counts, the short fix-first
list, and the not-checked line. No revised draft.
