# Fact-bound personal letter

A letter written for yourself can do real harm when the draft adds a job you did not
hold, a hardship that did not happen or a sentence copied from your resume. The reader
may verify it and the writer signs it. The draft holds only what the person supplied.

Based on ten first-person reports of job, admission, appeal, immigration and notice
letters; treat the steps as a checklist.

## Steps

1. List what was supplied: facts, dates, names, numbers, documents, the reader, the
   goal, the deadline and the format limit. Write the list before drafting.
2. If the person asks to say something the list does not support (a medical reason, a
   skill, a past employer), do not assert it. Say so, ask for the fact or the document,
   and either leave a placeholder or offer wording that only states what they gave.
3. Draft only from the list. Each missing fact is a visible placeholder in the form
   `[NEEDED: what, from whom or which document]`. Never invent circumstances, jobs,
   dates, quotes, reference numbers or events.
4. Keep the person's own phrasing where they gave any. Cut sentences copied word for
   word from the source material (resume bullets, the posting, the notice) and
   restate the point in the letter's own words.
   Enclosures, offers and stock closing lines are statements too: name an enclosure
   only if the person said they have that document, otherwise write
   `[NEEDED: enclosure, if you have it]`; do not write "a copy of the permit is
   enclosed", "I can attend a hearing" or "send your decision to the address above"
   unless it was supplied.
   A circumstance the person wants to claim but has not supported stays out of the
   letter body entirely: no sentence may lean on it ("in light of these
   circumstances", "given what happened"). Write a letter that stands on the
   supported facts alone, and offer the unsupported point separately, below the
   letter, as an optional paragraph with `[NEEDED: ...]` slots to add only if true
   and documented. Do not pre-write the story for the person.
5. Add a facts-used table after the letter: one row for each statement in the letter
   (facts, the request, the enclosures), the fact or document it comes from, and a
   row for each claim left out as unsupported. A statement with no row is removed.
6. For appeals, benefit, immigration, tenancy, employment, fine or other legal-type
   letters: add the evidence map (each point to the document that would prove it)
   and say that a qualified person (lawyer, caseworker, advisor) or the issuing
   authority's own guidance must check the letter before it is sent. State what you
   could not verify: rules, deadlines, eligibility.
7. Check and list, as separate items: the deadline, the required attachments, where
   and how to submit, and any length or format limit. Say which of these the person
   must confirm with the issuing office, school or employer.

## Output

1. Supplied-facts list.
2. The draft with placeholders.
3. Facts-used table.
4. Open items: unsupported claims not asserted, missing facts, attachments, deadline,
   and, for legal or benefit letters, the line that a qualified person verifies.

The letter never claims the person is eligible, will win or is entitled to anything.
